I recommend three constructions within one replacement pipeline: body-derived fitted garments, sewn panels driven by a small deformable cloth mesh for loose garments, and anatomically attached rigid ornaments. They should share body measurements, appearance variants and explicit outfit relationships. The present combination of outline sweeps, transferred weights, accumulated rest-pose pushes and inferred cover volumes should be retired.

The evidence supports a structural rebuild. The skirt and wrap constructors surround both legs with one convex outline. `skirt_weights` then removes shin, foot and toe influence below the crotch, transfers it to the thighs, and blends weights around the garment. A cloth skirt needs freedom between the legs; this construction instead averages their skeletal movements. The horizontal hems in kneeling and digging are consistent with that choice. Cloaks include the arms in their hanging outline, yet cannot move aside when an arm swings through them. Rest-pose displacement cannot resolve both situations.

There are also problems independent of those shapes:

- Trousers are three overlapping disconnected tubes, not a joined seat and two legs. Shirts and robes have separate sleeves and yokes; their armholes are subsequently made by deleting intersecting triangles, including sleeve triangles.
- Garments are unskinned using their full weight vectors but subsequently drawn with four influences. Unless those vectors already have at most four nonzero weights, the inverse construction and the rendered deformation differ.
- Garment morphs initially retain a fixed spatial offset from the adult body surface. That offset does not automatically scale, rotate or redrape with a different body. Later settling changes the result but supplies no general solution for all combinations of age, sex and build.
- The layer model cannot express several actual outfits. Hood and cloak have the same layer; so do sling and cloak, and apron and loin flap. Their relative coverage is therefore excluded from ordinary inner-mask construction. `wrapLong-chest` is classified as a hip garment despite its chest attachment.
- `settle_layers` considers potential pairs across the garment catalogue, rather than only generated combinations. An item can therefore be displaced to accommodate something never worn with it.
- Runtime mask decoding combines hide bits per vertex before deciding whether a triangle disappears. Different garments can hide different corners and collectively delete a triangle that neither garment covers completely.
- `pipeline.py` still raises an exception for penetration values over tolerance. This directly contradicts the requested information-only status.

These are reasons to change the construction, not proof that each observed pixel has a particular cause.

For **trousers, fitted shirt bodies, fitted sleeves, breast cloths and cloth bands**, I would derive the garment’s contact regions from the *rendered* body topology. Preserve explicit correspondence to body vertices, the final four skin weights and every applicable body morph. Construct sewn garment surfaces with intentional ease and finished boundaries; do not begin with independent convex rings. Trousers need a connected crotch and seat. Sleeves need an actual armhole joined to the yoke, with deliberately authored shoulder-to-arm deformation.

Inside those fitted regions, the garment becomes the visible replacement surface and the corresponding body faces are omitted. This is ordinary clothing construction, not a generic concealment layer: the surface must have the garment’s shape, material, seams and silhouette. Keep the exposed body at necklines, cuffs and trouser hems, including enough continuation inside each opening to withstand bending. Matching seam vertices must receive identical positions, morphs and weights.

This removes the principal opportunity for the body to escape a fitted trouser leg: the covered surface and its garment follow the same anatomical deformation. It also avoids retaining a second skin surface that can shimmer through it. It does **not** prove the reported failure fixed; a runtime transform, binding or mask error could still affect the replacement asset.

A breast cloth needs a tensioned front panel, fitted side and back sections, and a lower edge close to the torso. Copying the complete breast surface would exchange the flange for a painted-on or bra-like appearance. Fit its shape separately to the actual female bodies, using a small panel control mesh where bridging is needed. A shirt’s lower portion is different again: the supplied shirt reaches nearly to the knee. Its hanging tail belongs to the loose-panel system, otherwise the digging lump merely becomes a different pelvis deformation.

Expected residual risks are tight-joint pinching, an excessively anatomical silhouette, abrupt transitions from fitted to loose cloth, and exposed boundary cracks. These require shaping and picture checks, not larger uniform offsets.

For **loin flaps, aprons and girdle tails**, use a fitted waistband with separately constructed front and back panels. Their attachment is tight; their hanging cloth is not. Pins follow the pelvis and waist, while each panel moves against the thighs and buttocks through the loose-cloth mechanism below. Do not rigidly bind the entire flap to the pelvis, and do not give its centre an averaged influence from both thighs. The Fang apron/loin-flap combinations need an explicit overlap arrangement.

This addresses the stationary front flap penetrated by a lifting thigh and the detached rear flap. It can introduce excessive flutter, a stiff-looking flap or contact chatter; damping and material-specific bending stiffness are therefore part of construction. Preserve the girdle-tail silhouette even though the inventory finds no defect in it.

For **short and knee skirts, long wraps, robe skirts and togas**, construct sewn panels with a close-fitting waist or chest attachment, controlled gathers, and an intentional hem. A child’s short skirt should be fitted to that child’s pelvis and buttocks, with modest room below; it should not be an adult outline plus a retained clearance. It must still read as a skirt, not shorts or a skin-tight tube. Wraps need an actual overlapping edge, with sufficient overlap during motion. Any slit must be appropriate to the garment rather than invented solely to let a knee escape.

Use a small, connected cloth control mesh beneath the render mesh. This control mesh has prescribed edge lengths, bending resistance and explicit pins. At each update, first evaluate the game’s complete body pose—including clip blending, stride modification, clavicles, lean, gaze and IK—then solve the cloth around that posed body. The collision body remains complete even where rendering hides the skin. Use swept contacts or equivalent continuous handling for fast limb movement, and collision constraints for faces and edges as well as vertices. Vertex-only collision can leave a thigh passing through the middle of a large triangle.

The render mesh follows the solved control mesh through fixed surface coordinates and local frames. It must not receive ordinary skeletal skinning a second time. Fitted-to-loose seams share exact boundary positions. Reconstruct normals from the resulting cloth shape; moving positions while retaining inappropriate normals can produce dark marks without a geometric hole.

I would make this a constrained, strongly damped deformation system, with little optional secondary motion. Its purpose is to fit cloth around the final pose, not to add theatrical flutter. Cloth updates must handle animation transitions, large frame intervals, spawning and teleportation without starting from an intersecting state. Use a posed initialization and contact-aware stepping; a simple snap to an earlier rest shape would restore the very failures being removed.

This construction targets the short-skirt box and digging shelf, knee-skirt double ring, long-wrap tears and gaping chest edge, robe holes and ground-level spread, and toga bagging. Kneeling and digging require contact with the ground as well as the body. A long robe cannot be both floor-length and arbitrarily pass through a deeply flexed leg: its panels must gather, slide or form an appropriate fold. Those shapes need deliberate art direction.

Expected new risks include solver jitter, stiff folds, visible stretching, overlap inversion, contact failures during fast transitions and intersections between the render surface and body even when control vertices are clear. Preserve folded-panel ordering, check self-contact where panels overlap, and validate the final rendered surface. No finite solver configuration is a mathematical guarantee of clean clothing in every possible pose; this architecture supplies the missing degrees of freedom and a way to correct the actual pose. The acceptance evidence remains the moving picture.

For **capes and cloaks**, construct their shoulder support and closure first, then hang panels from it. Do not wrap a convex hull around the entire arm envelope. Their arms must have an intended route: beneath a movable panel, through an appropriate opening, or outside a genuinely open edge. Hidden forearms are not an acceptable substitute for moving the cloth.

A chest-worn cape must cover the prescribed upper body through arm raises without becoming a circular collar. One-shoulder capes need a supported diagonal edge and a fitted or tensioned sash section, rather than a horizontal waist loop. Cloaks need cloth spanning the chest naturally, not female breast morphs transferred directly into two projecting bulges. The shoulder support follows the relevant chest and clavicle motion; the free cloth uses the same collision-aware control mesh. This addresses cape arm penetration, cloak forearm penetration, shoulder stand-off, sash loops and digging flare. Its principal risks are shoulder pinching, insufficient arm clearance and panels that look implausibly rigid.

**Layering must be an outfit relationship, not a global slot number.** Build a directed relationship for each actual co-worn pair and, where necessary, for regions of a garment. Hood shoulder cloth can lie under a cloak while its head section remains exposed; an infant carrier has a different relationship to the cloak behind it. Waist beads may sit against skin, be concealed, or lie visibly over a particular garment. One universal “ornaments are innermost” rule is insufficient.

Remove only body or inner-garment faces that remain safely concealed throughout the supported movement. Preserve the full collision representation and all surfaces visible through openings. Around moving openings, resolve contact and overlap geometrically instead of permanently deleting additional anatomy. Where a static omission is safe, derive it from explicit garment regions and actual outfit relationships, with face boundaries suited to the visible edge. Do not retain the universal inward-normal push as the seam-fixing mechanism.

Keep the seasonal robe/hood/cloak combinations. They are expressly required. Conceal their interior regions while retaining genuine collars, hems and openings, and solve the exposed interfaces. Do not inflate the outer cloak around the entire unseen robe. Conversely, everyday open capes and open cloaks must not receive an inner top forbidden by §19.15. An adult woman wearing such an outfit still needs the outer garment itself to provide the prescribed coverage. If a construction cannot satisfy both rules, revise that garment’s supported wearing arrangement within the attested dress, or exclude that specific arrangement; do not relax either rule.

For **hoods and veils**, separate head support from shoulder drape. The hood crown follows the head; its neck transition and shoulder panels follow the neck/chest and deform against the underlying garment. Its opening and overlap are authored boundaries. The veil’s lower neck section must overlap the robe or cloak collar through head turns and shoulder extremes, while its face section follows the head. The dark neck band may involve geometry, exposed skin or shading; the written record cannot determine which. Likewise, a uniform dark head at 3 m is a readability problem that may require material and opening adjustments as well as construction. Preserve the visible eye opening and intended silhouette.

For **ornaments**, replace the chest shell and jagged shoulder fragments with deliberately arranged beadwork. However, the outfit list shows `neckBeads-chest` as the only upper covering for Zulu youth and Mongo women. Turning it into sparse necklace strings would violate the coverage rule. Those cases need an opaque, sufficiently covering beaded construction consistent with the existing dress interpretation, or a researched same-people replacement. The attached research excerpts do not authorize choosing that replacement.

Neck strings should follow fitted paths on the neck/chest with limited deformation, and waist beads should follow the intended waist path and actual layer. Individual beads should preserve their shape. Wrist and ankle rings should be rigidly attached to their respective forearm or shin segments, oriented around the limb axis and sized per body. A ring must not average hand and forearm motion across its whole circumference. Combine rings into shared geometry where material permits; one rigid attachment does not require one draw call.

Retain the successful cap, head ring, topknot, hair bag, headband and turban designs, but bring them through the same shape-fitting and attachment contract. Their absence of defects at sheet scale does not establish their clearance under a moving hood. No redesign is warranted without new evidence.

The **baby sling** needs an actual support arrangement: fitted straps or wrapped cloth, an exterior pouch, and an identifiable infant positioned relative to the wearer’s back and shoulders. The infant can remain rigid relative to an upper-torso carrier frame; its head must not receive the wearer’s head rotation. Fit the cloak around the carrier relationship so the infant is not swallowed by the outer layer. Check front, side and rear views in locomotion and digging. The remaining risk is a stiff or poorly seated infant; the blue lump is not resolved merely by moving a sphere upward.

The cost is substantial but can be bounded. The following are initial engineering budgets, not measurements of this repository:

| Area | Pipeline and authoring cost | Runtime target and main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Fitted trousers, tops, sleeves and breast cloths | Moderate: establish topology correspondence, sew seams and fit each supported body family; much work is reusable | A few hundred to roughly 1,500 triangles per major piece; ordinary four-weight skinning; no cloth solve for fitted regions |
| Flaps, tails and short skirts | Moderate: panel shapes, attachments and contact tuning | Roughly 40–120 control vertices and 200–800 render triangles per piece |
| Robes, long wraps, togas, capes and cloaks | High: panel topology, motion fitting, overlap and ground contact | Roughly 100–300 control vertices and 600–2,000 render triangles per substantial piece; contact solving is the dominant new CPU cost |
| Hoods, veils and sling | Moderate to high because of interfaces and visibility | Small rigid or skinned regions plus roughly 30–120 control vertices where drape is needed |
| Beads, rings and retained head pieces | Low to moderate; chest beadwork needs more art direction | Tens to hundreds of triangles per assembly, grouped by material; rigid pieces need no cloth solve |

Start with roughly 4–8 solver iterations and adaptive substeps, then measure and tune against pictures and actual crowd timings. As an illustration of scale, 30 visible people with two 200-vertex panels each, eight iterations and two substeps already produce 192,000 control-vertex iteration visits per frame, before counting edge constraints and contact work. It would be misleading to promise a millisecond budget from the attachments.

The render mesh can use shared topology with per-person control data. Keep garment identity separate from render partitions: splitting fitted and loose portions or choosing a fit variant need not create extra logical garment bits. Draw cost should ordinarily remain approximately one draw per material group, but splitting deformation mechanisms can add draws unless they share a supported material path. Both backend implementations must be measured. Geometry and update simplification may vary with distance, but necessary coverage and contact correction must work at every graphics level.

Pipeline generation should become cheaper than repeated worst-pose expansion and mask searches once templates exist, but fitting and verification will still be expensive. Cache templates, shape fits, attachment data and outfit relationships independently. Expect several engineer and technical-artist weeks for the common deformation/rendering work and additional weeks for fitting and visual review of all garment families; this is not a few-parameter repair. Actual elapsed builds, asset size, memory, draw counts and crowd timings are required before final budgets can be accepted.

Keep MakeHuman body generation, the anatomical skeleton, existing motion and the glTF asset route unless the missing files reveal a specific incompatibility. Replace garment construction, deformation bindings, coverage metadata and their runtime consumption together. Retire the old garment constructors and fit/mask repair loops, their obsolete configuration, and the old asset schema at cutover. Geometry utilities and diagnostic reports may survive where still useful; a selectable old garment path may not.

Finally, verification must go beyond the supplied inventory. It omits build variation, samples only some runtime situations, and did not capture the walking-trouser report. Force the relevant outfits into reproducible game scenes rather than wait for random walkers. Re-shot side views must include the reported trouser case and the child short-skirt case, full motion and transitions, both backends, village and port lighting, and normal viewing distances. Inspect all 158 recorded combinations plus any additional combinations revealed by the actual generator’s branches. The hovering Bambara figure, detached pieces and Hausa spots need ownership and transform tracing before they can be attributed to a garment.
